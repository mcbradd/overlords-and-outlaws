# Family-game design gate

Binding user direction, 17 September 2026. Applies to all current and future game design, teaching, cards, art and interface work. It supersedes earlier acceptance language where that language permits explanations to compensate for a confusing mechanic.

Overlords & Outlaws is a family game. Players must be able to understand its language at a sixth-grade reading level. Minimize text while keeping the mechanic and its purpose clear. **A move that needs multiple sentences of explanation to become understandable fails the design gate.** Simplify the rule, the decision or its visible presentation instead of adding paragraphs, tooltips or more tutorial steps.

## Required design practice

**All-mechanics review mandate:** The user explicitly extends this freedom to every mechanic standing in the way of fun, clear gameplay. Nothing is protected by precedent, sunk implementation effort, designer suggestions or a previous panel decision. Succession and its clock, rank/suit rules, Trade, marriage, defense, passing, card circulation and external pressure all must earn their complexity. The physical family-game premise and current user goals guide the review; specific mechanisms are candidates, not sacred requirements. Replace or remove them when a better-supported alternative improves fun, meaningful choices and clarity.

- Use familiar, concrete words and consistent action names. Teach necessary game terms through visible examples before relying on them.
- Show the relevant card, rank, suit, target and result together. Short labels and a clear visual example should carry the instruction.
- Give one idea at a time. An action prompt may use one short sentence; this is a ceiling, not a target. Several fragments that hide a paragraph's worth of rules also fail.
- Preserve meaningful uncertainty and choices. Remove bookkeeping, exceptions and memory burdens that do not earn their teaching cost.
- Keep the win condition, current goal and reason for the next action visible and simple. A player should not need a paragraph to connect a move to the goal.
- Reference material may document edge cases, but cannot rescue an ordinary move that is unclear at the table. Internal engineering specifications may remain detailed; player-facing complexity must not inherit that detail.

## Acceptance tests

1. Review every mechanic for the amount of explanation needed, not just the length of its current copy. If ordinary use needs multiple explanatory sentences, reopen the design before implementation.
2. Inventory every player-facing prompt and description. Flag multiple-sentence gameplay explanations, unfamiliar terms, nested conditions and duplicated labels. Readability scores may assist; short text and proper names make them unreliable as sole approval.
3. Inspect actual rendered cards and screens. A visual must explain the action rather than decorate a text-heavy rule. Symbols must be introduced and cannot merely replace words with a code to memorize.
4. A fresh screen-only learner must explain the immediate choice and expected result from the current presentation, then explain the game objective in plain words. Record misunderstanding and additional explanation required. Do not coach the answer.
5. Test with families and sixth-grade readers when available. Agent reviews and reading-level checks are proxies, and must be labeled as such; they cannot certify family comprehension or enjoyment.

The correction cycle must challenge the present succession clock, exact Trade exceptions, Queen-role versus Q-rank distinction, marriage conditions and repeated passing against this gate. Existing implementation is not a reason to retain a rule.
