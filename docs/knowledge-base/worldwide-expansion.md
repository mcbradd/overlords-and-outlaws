# Worldwide Expansion V1.1 — complete distillation

Source: `OO Worldwide Expansion V1.1.pdf`, 12 pages. The filename says V1.1 while its cover says world / IP / product expansion v0.1; preserve this discrepancy rather than treating either as resolved.

## Pages 1–2 — expansion thesis

Every culture changes the game. The concept is global from the outset. Inheritance, marriage, alliance, rivalry, succession, and collapse recur across history; different cultures answer them differently. Archives must introduce culturally grounded people, institutions, and pressures that alter play. A shared engine supports compatibility while cultural specificity preserves interest.

## Page 3 — first archive

**Sanguinis Caerulei**, European Blue Blood, is the initial doorway. The deck claims over 200 dynasties have been identified, but supplies no full list or supporting dataset. Famous houses, disputed heirs, marriages, rival branches, restorations, and collapse provide extensive expansion potential. The Alba card-sheet exhibit connects this future to the core game.

## Page 4 — worldwide territory

- Courts and Caliphates: Moorish dynasties, scholarship, diplomacy, trade, competing authority centers.
- Clans and Shogunates: Japanese houses, sworn duty, adoption, succession, contested legitimacy.
- Khans and Confederations: steppe lineages, mobility, tribute, coalition, imperial fracture.
- Kingdoms of the World: African, American, Indian, and other cultures developed on their own historical terms.

## Page 5 — what earns an archive

Historical identity: recognizable rulers and bloodlines grounded in place, period, and political order. Distinct pressure: a culturally appropriate system changes what players value, risk, reveal, or protect. Cross-play value: new interactions remain compatible with the dynastic engine. Names and art alone are insufficient.

## Page 6 — research questions, not finished rules

Viking houses: mobility, raiding, assembly law, oath networks. Mongol lineages: confederation, tribute, succession, rapid alliances and sharper collapse. Indian dynasties: plural courts, trade networks, regional sovereignty and layered authority. Samurai clans: adoption, duty, household loyalty, legitimacy. All are proposed research lanes rather than claims that these cultures can be reduced to those traits.

## Page 7 — crossover promise

Hypothetical combinations: Viking + Mongol creates a volatile race around mobility, tribute, territory, and exposed authority. Indian + Moorish may reward coalitions, court influence, commerce, and layered rule. Samurai + European may introduce unfamiliar adoption, marriage, and succession choices. These are speculative interactions, not tested features.

## Page 8 — constant and variable

Constant engine: Draft → Declare → Build → Barter → Seize → Marry → Betray → Survive history. Variable: culture changes how power moves through roles, institutions, obligations, resources, and disruptions. The aim is one compatible rules spine with many political worlds.

## Page 9 — product architecture

1. Core archive: complete playable entry in one historical world.
2. Dynasty expansions: additional houses, branches, people, conflicts.
3. Cultural archives: different political systems compatible with the engine.
4. Crossover play: combine archives and discover new interactions.

## Page 10 — property formats

Trading cards carry collectible dynasties, figures, events, and archive-specific rules. Boxed editions curate conflicts and provide entry points. Digital play provides enforcement, global matchmaking, and expansion. Story worlds include publishing, documentary context, screen development, and character-led lore. All return to the question of holding power. These are future ambitions, not requirements for the approved single-player prototype.

## Page 11 — development mandate

Research before rules; avoid stereotypes and borrowed fantasy shorthand. Use historians, cultural advisers, and specialist artists where deeper authority is required. Mechanics should derive from historical operation of power. Each archive must work alone and create meaningful mixed-archive interactions. The prototype does not claim specialist cultural review has occurred.

## Page 12 — future promise

The initial 200-plus European-house claim is only a starting point. The deck names Moorish dynasties, samurai clans, Mongol khanates, African kingdoms, Indian empires, and Indigenous nations and empires across North and South America. Each should bring its own political logic. “Infinite combinations” is aspirational marketing language, not a literal finite-card-system property.
