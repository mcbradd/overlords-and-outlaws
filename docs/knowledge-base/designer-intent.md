# Designer correspondence — supplied by the user, 15 September 2026

## Design authority

The designer describes a historically grounded, competitive game accessible to a child, parent, and grandparent at the same table. Competition, history, and collecting beautiful historical people should each offer a reason to play.

The central proposition is that accumulating power makes it harder to hold. More territory means more borders; more heirs mean more claims. A marriage can save a dynasty or destroy it. The designer compares this to a steel rod: a short rod resists bending, while an extremely long rod becomes vulnerable. Legacy can be a burden rather than a privilege, an idea connected to the designer's book *The Weight of the Crown*.

Becoming king is less important than what happens afterward. Players should build Houses, bargain over marriages, steal Royals, conceal options, choose what to expose, and survive shared history. Interregna and Eudoxia add pressure while players contest power. Expansion should expose vulnerabilities rather than merely increase a score.

Cards are collectible objects: real historical figures, genealogy, heraldry, beautiful art, and discoverable historical details. They should feel like members of a bloodline rather than disposable combat machinery. Detailed history must be sourced; invented mechanical abilities must be labeled as game design.

The designer explicitly describes the source as a work in progress, without proper playtesting. Some mechanics remain fluid; most numbers are educated starting guesses. Digital implementation should test these assumptions rather than treat provisional numbers as authoritative.

## Consequences for revision 2

- Retain an explicit claim-and-hold victory; do not reduce the game to defeating an opponent's health bar.
- Make the costs of a large court and dependence on marriage visible.
- Preserve concealed hands, royal seizure, political defense, economic choices, shared historical pressures, and the Witness.
- Explain each contest locally: attack, defense response, consequence, and strategic reason.
- Support two to four Houses throughout the rules, default to three for solo teaching, and provide two-to-four-player local family play with private handoffs. Each family player chooses a distinct House. Online play is outside this revision.
- Keep the basic turn small: two orders, a limited court, clear gold costs, one defense response per rival turn.
- Treat claims of emergent depth and family accessibility as hypotheses needing actual play evidence.
