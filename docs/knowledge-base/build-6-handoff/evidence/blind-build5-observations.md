## What this experience is for, in my own words
A competitive card-table game about assembling a ruling family and keeping a successor in power while rivals bargain for or Recall the people you need. It is a race to build a stable succession, not a battle to remove all opponents. Cards are both people on the table and resources in hand: playing one can help the family but remove a defense. A low native person can be a good heir because the target rank does not itself resist Recall; keeping higher same-Dynasty cards hidden matters more. Public Played cards teach me who will regain threats next round. I can reason about known exchanges, visible courts and Crown requirements, but new draws and untouched hand identities stay uncertain.

The opening tutorial taught the legal motions but did not teach choosing among real alternatives. Ordinary play supplied that lesson: early all-native hands tempt a claim before enough defense exists; foreign cards can delay opponents or purchase my own native threats back; an attack gives its lead to the victim if successful, so captures can strengthen the other family's future hand. Repeated passes near a protected win are meaningful, but visually feel repetitive in three/four-player games while unrelated rival actions reset the round end.

## Separate assessment: family sixth-grade reader and minimal text
This is an adult screen-only playtester assessment, not a test with children. I do not think the present ordinary screen lets a new sixth-grade reader confidently understand every move with minimal text. The difficulty is often the underlying decision model and missing visible relationships, not just long sentences.

- Recruit is the clearest ordinary move. Select a native card, confirm it joins Court; when no ruler exists, recruitment restores one. But "native", "Dynasty", "Court", "ruler" and "supporter" need grounding. The face says Recruit even on foreign cards whose live controls do not allow recruitment; a child must distinguish printed possibilities from current legal actions. The tradeoff of exposing a defense is not evident from merely seeing a Recruit button.
- Recall is the largest comprehension problem. The ordinary word does not explain an attack/exchange. I needed multiple rules together: match the TARGET'S Dynasty, target rank does not matter, only its owner answers, answer must beat the LEAD, equal fails, Ace beats only J/Q/K, and successful exchange sends both cards to new owners' Played until next round. The prompt "Defend with a higher card of that Dynasty, or an Ace against J, Q or K" helps choose a legal answer but does not by itself explain the exchange. Legal filtering let me act before I could fully predict the result. This is a design problem in comparing and moving components, not solvable only by trimming prose.
- Defend becomes easy after learning Recall: choose the lowest adequate native card and confirm. But the relationship is attacker lead versus answer, not attacker's number versus target's number. Both the target and the hand show ranks, so the important comparison competes with irrelevant numbers. A clear visible lead/answer comparison would be more useful than additional explanatory sentences. Ace's conditional ordering needs an explicit simple visual reference.
- Trade asks for unfamiliar full historical names in a large dropdown, usually without visible rank/Dynasty beside the requested name. I could use exact names mainly after seeing public exchanges. A new reader cannot easily reason from an unfamiliar name to a rank or family; this is a lookup/recognition problem, not reading stamina. The rules then branch: opponent may decline without proof; both cards wait until next round; only a declared lower native received by the offerer may recruit now; one offer per recipient per round. A short button cannot communicate all that. The preview needs visible card identities and destinations, plus a clearly separate immediate-recruit exception. Decline trade leading to heading/button "Let it happen" / "Confirm Let it happen" is a direct meaning mismatch.
- Name heir sounds like naming an existing court member, but the action plays a hand card. It starts a multi-stage timing contract: keep old ruler, heir and original supporter until next round; then keep the new ruler and that same supporter for the entire following round. The named status banner is helpful, but "next round" and "full round" require a learned timing model. The original supporter cannot simply be replaced. This needs persistent visible stages/links, not merely a shorter paragraph. The old ruler remaining in Court after transfer creates another person whose importance has changed.
- Marry & name heir combines eligibility, a card move, a pair, and victory timing. The Queen is a character role, not necessarily rankQ. Foreign card must be equal or adjacent in rank; Ace/King are not adjacent. The foreign spouse still has its original Dynasty for Recall and needs the Queen to stay. I needed several sentences and the tutorial example to understand the legal opportunity. In actual game6 it opened an extra suit of vulnerability. A family reader needs the pair and allowed rank comparison represented on the table; labels alone do not explain it.
- Pass looks elementary but its meaning is unusually subtle: it does not remove me from the round, I can defend and act later, another play resets consecutive passes, and only every player's consecutive pass finishes the round. Returning Played cards, drawing one and rotating the starting seat happen together. Long multi-player rounds made repeated Pass prompts feel like the game was not advancing, although it was legal. The end-round condition should be legible as shared public progress.
- Outcome language needs polish: "You claims", "You wins" and continued "To act" at a terminal result weaken confidence. The main terminal message does state who won and why. Dense three/four-player whole-table views make card names and counters too small for a reader to track without inspection, while hand cards stay large. This is a presentation/attention problem beyond copy length.

Minimal-text priorities from this play experience: show the two cards being compared for Recall/defense; show exchange origins/destinations and when cards return; show named ruler/supporter/heir links and two succession stages; show how many consecutive passes remain; put rank/Dynasty beside trade choices. Keep the detailed reference available, but do not require recalling a paragraph to interpret an ordinary move.

## Agency assessment across the ten
Most wins involved meaningful timing, not merely clicking the first enabled action. Useful repeated decisions: retain high native defense, claim with a lower native, wait for an opponent to spend defense, trade known native threats away, distinguish old ruler from required new ruler after succession, and pass rather than spend irrelevant foreign cards when winning. But exact-name trade offers were often the obvious way to strip threats, and AI acceptance made that reliable once a card was known. Two losses differed: game6 involved my premature/exposed claims and poor timing; game9 provided almost no useful counterplay because every draw was native. No claim of balance or family preference follows from this one ten-game player cohort.
